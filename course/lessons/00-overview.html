0. The theorem, and why it is a miracle

Over the complex numbers, the deepest facts about the cohomology of an algebraic variety — that the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence degenerates, that ample line bundles kill cohomology (Kodaira vanishing) — are analytic. Their classical proofs use harmonic forms, Kähler metrics, the ∂∂̄-lemma. Deligne and Illusie found a proof that uses none of this. Instead it runs through characteristic \(p\), and turns on a single, almost absurdly cheap-looking input: the variety should lift one infinitesimal step, from characteristic \(p\) to "characteristic \(p^2\)". This lesson explains the statement, the strategy, and why the existence of such a lift has any right to control cohomology at all.

The object of study

Let \(X\) be a smooth proper scheme over a field \(k\). Its algebraic de Rham cohomology is the hypercohomology of the de Rham complex, \[ H^{*}_{\dR}(X/k) := \mathbb{H}^{*}(X, \Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}), \qquad \Om^{\bullet}_{X/k} : \; \cO_X \xto{d} \Om^1_{X/k} \xto{d} \Om^2_{X/k} \to \cdots \] The "stupid" filtration of this complex by \(\Om^{\geqslant i}\) gives the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence \[ \tag{0.1} E_1^{ij} = H^j(X, \Om^i_{X/k}) \;\Longrightarrow\; H^{i+j}_{\dR}(X/k). \] To say it degenerates at \(E_1\) is to say all differentials vanish, equivalently that for every \(n\), \[ \sum_{i+j=n} \dim_k H^j(X,\Om^i_{X/k}) \;=\; \dim_k H^n_{\dR}(X/k). \] The numbers \(h^{ij}=\dim H^j(X,\Om^i)\) are the Hodge numbers; degeneration says they add up, without loss, to the de Rham Betti numbers. We unpack this in Lesson 1.

Why this is not obvious

A spectral sequence is a machine for losing information: each differential \(d_r\) is a potential drop in dimension. Degeneration says the machine never fires. Over \(\C\) this is a theorem of Hodge theory. Over a field of characteristic \(p\) it can genuinely fail (Lesson 14) — so any proof must use a real hypothesis, and the question is which one.

The main theorem

Work now over a perfect field \(k\) of characteristic \(p>0\). Frobenius \(a\mapsto a^p\) is a morphism of schemes; from \(X\) it produces a "twisted" copy \(X'\) (the Frobenius twist, Lesson 2) together with the relative Frobenius \(F : X \to X'\), a finite flat \(k\)-morphism. Pushing the de Rham complex forward along \(F\) gives a complex \(F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\) of \(\cO_{X'}\)-modules. The Cartier isomorphism (Lesson 3) identifies its cohomology sheaves: \(\cH^i F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k} \cong \Om^i_{X'/k}\).

The "characteristic \(p^2\)" input is a lift of \(X\) over the Witt vectors \(W_2(k)\) — a flat \(W_2(k)\)-scheme \(\tX\) reducing to \(X\) mod \(p\) (Lesson 4). Here is the heart of the paper.

Theorem (2.1) (Deligne–Illusie)

Each smooth lift \(\tX\) of \(X\) over \(W_2(k)\) determines, canonically, an isomorphism in the derived category \(D(X')\) \[ \varphi_{\tX} : \bigoplus_{i<p} \Om^i_{X'/k}[-i] \;\iso\; \tau_{<p}\, F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k} \] inducing the Cartier isomorphism on each cohomology sheaf.

In words: up to degree \(p\), the pushed-forward de Rham complex splits as the direct sum of its cohomology sheaves — it is as simple as a complex can be, a sum of shifted sheaves with no differentials linking them — and a lift mod \(p^2\) is exactly the datum that produces the splitting. The truncation \(\tau_{<p}\) and the range \(i<p\) are not blemishes; they are where the whole subject lives, and Lesson 7 explains why.

What you get for free

Once the complex splits, cohomology cannot help but add up. A short count of dimensions (Lesson 9) gives:

Corollary (2.4)

If \(X\) is proper smooth over \(k\) of dimension \(< p\) (in fact \(\leqslant p\)) and lifts over \(W_2(k)\), then the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence (0.1) degenerates at \(E_1\).

From this single characteristic-\(p\) statement, two cornerstones of complex geometry fall out by formal arguments:

  • Degeneration in characteristic \(0\) (Cor. 2.7): spread a complex variety out over a finitely generated ring, reduce modulo a large prime \(p\) — the reduction automatically lifts mod \(p^2\) and has dimension \(<p\) — apply 2.4, and lift the conclusion back. No Hodge theory. (Lesson 10.)
  • Kodaira–Akizuki–Nakano vanishing (Cor. 2.8, due to Raynaud; and 2.11 in char 0): \(H^j(X,\Om^i\otimes L^{-1})=0\) for \(i+j<\inf(p,\dim X)\) when \(L\) is ample. The proof is a Frobenius descent that has nothing to do with metrics. (Lesson 11.)

Why a lift mod p² has any right to do this

Here is the whole idea in miniature; the details are Lesson 6. Suppose, optimistically, that not only \(X\) but the relative Frobenius \(F:X\to X'\) lifts: we have \(\tF:\tX \to \tX'\) over \(W_2(k)\) covering \(F\). On differentials, \(F^*=0\) (Frobenius multiplies by \(p\), which is \(0\) in characteristic \(p\)). So the lift \(\tF^* : \Om^1_{\tX'} \to \tF_*\Om^1_{\tX}\) lands in the subsheaf \(p\,\tF_*\Om^1_{\tX}\) — it is "secretly divisible by \(p\)". Dividing, \[ f := \tfrac{1}{p}\,\tF^* : \Om^1_{X'/k} \longrightarrow F_*\Om^1_{X/k}. \] A one-line computation (write \(\tF^*(x) = x^p + p\,u(x)\)) gives \[ f(dx) = x^{p-1}\,dx + d\,u(x), \] whose class in \(\cH^1\) is exactly \(x^{p-1}dx\) — the Cartier isomorphism. Dividing the lift of Frobenius by \(p\) literally manufactures the splitting map. The lift mod \(p^2\) is what makes the division legal; the rest of the paper is the (real) work of doing this when \(F\) only lifts locally, and of organizing the local pieces into a global statement in the derived category.

The hypothesis is on \(X'\), and it is sufficient, not necessary

Two warnings the paper is careful about. (i) What must lift is \(X'\), not \(X\); over a perfect field the two are abstractly isomorphic so this is invisible, but in families it matters (Lesson 13). (ii) Liftability is a sufficient condition for degeneration, not a necessary one: Raynaud built surfaces that do not lift mod \(p^2\) yet whose spectral sequence still degenerates (Lesson 14). The sharp statement — that liftability of \(X'\) is equivalent to splittability of the truncation \(\tau_{\leqslant 1}\) — is the gerbe theorem of §3 (Lesson 12).

A first sanity check: curves

Example — the simplest case

Let \(X\) be a smooth proper curve over \(k\), so \(\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k} = (\cO_X \xto{d} \Om^1_{X/k})\) lives only in degrees \(0,1\). For any prime \(p\geqslant 2\) we have \(\{0,1\} \subset \{i : i<p\}\), so the truncation \(\tau_{<p}\) is the whole complex and Theorem 2.1 already gives a full splitting \( \cO_{X'}\oplus \Om^1_{X'}[-1] \iso F_*\Om^{\bullet}_X \). Curves always lift to \(W_2(k)\) (the obstruction lives in \(H^2\) of a curve, which vanishes). Hence the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence of any smooth proper curve degenerates at \(E_1\): concretely, \[ \dim H^1_{\dR}(X/k) = \dim H^0(X,\Om^1) + \dim H^1(X,\cO_X) = g + g = 2g. \] Every theorem in the paper specializes, on curves, to something you already believe — a good place to keep returning to.

Historical orientation

Degeneration in characteristic \(0\) was classically deduced from Hodge theory (and, for proper non-projective \(X\), Chow's lemma plus resolution of singularities). The first proof avoiding Hodge theory was Faltings', via \(p\)-adic Hodge theory (Hodge–Tate decompositions). Kato, then Fontaine–Messing, proved degeneration in characteristic \(p\) under a liftability hypothesis using crystalline methods, and re-derived the characteristic-\(0\) statement. Deligne and Illusie's contribution is an elementary and more precise result — the explicit splitting \(\varphi_{\tX}\) — from which all of the above follows by formal nonsense and dimension counts. The seed, as they note, is an observation of Mazur, made explicit in work of Ogus and Kato.

Warm-up exercises
  1. For \(X=\Pp^n_k\), the Hodge numbers are \(h^{ij}=1\) if \(i=j\leqslant n\) and \(0\) otherwise. Assuming degeneration, compute all \(\dim H^m_{\dR}(\Pp^n/k)\). (You will re-derive these honestly in Lesson 1.)
  2. Convince yourself that "the spectral sequence (0.1) degenerates at \(E_1\)" is equivalent to the single numerical identity \(\sum_{i+j=n} h^{ij} = \dim H^n_{\dR}\) for all \(n\), using only that the abutment is filtered with the \(E_\infty^{ij}\) as graded pieces and \(\dim E_\infty^{ij} \leqslant \dim E_1^{ij}\).
  3. Where, in the miniature argument above, did we use that we are working modulo \(p^2\) rather than modulo some \(p^m\) with \(m\geqslant 3\), or over all of \(W(k)\)? (Answer in Lesson 6: we divide by \(p\) exactly once.)
