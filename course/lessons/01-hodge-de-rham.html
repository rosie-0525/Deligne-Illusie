1. de Rham cohomology and the Hodge spectral sequence

Before we can ask whether something degenerates, we need the object that might. This lesson builds it from scratch: the algebraic de Rham complex, its hypercohomology, and the spectral sequence that relates the two — the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence (0.1). The punchline is a single numerical statement. There is always an inequality \(\sum_{i+j=n} h^{ij} \geqslant \dim H^n_{\dR}\); the Hodge numbers on the left are, in a precise sense, an overcount of de Rham cohomology. "Degeneration" is the assertion that the overcount is exact. Over \(\C\) it always is, by Hodge theory. In characteristic \(p\) it can fail — and the whole paper is the story of when it does not.

The algebraic de Rham complex

Let \(X\) be a smooth scheme over a field \(k\). The sheaf of Kähler differentials \(\Om^1_{X/k}\) is generated, locally, by symbols \(df\) for \(f\in\cO_X\), subject to \(k\)-linearity and the Leibniz rule \(d(fg)=f\,dg+g\,df\). Its exterior powers \(\Om^i_{X/k}=\bigwedge^i \Om^1_{X/k}\) come with a unique \(k\)-linear exterior derivative \[ \cO_X \xto{d} \Om^1_{X/k} \xto{d} \Om^2_{X/k} \xto{d} \cdots \] characterized by \(d(\omega\wedge\eta)=d\omega\wedge\eta+(-1)^{\deg\omega}\,\omega\wedge d\eta\) and \(d\circ d=0\). The \(\cO_X\)-modules \(\Om^i_{X/k}\) are not stalks of a constant sheaf and the maps \(d\) are not \(\cO_X\)-linear (they are only \(k\)-linear); the pair \((\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k},d)\) is a complex of sheaves of \(k\)-vector spaces. When \(X/k\) is smooth of dimension \(n\), each \(\Om^i_{X/k}\) is locally free of rank \(\binom{n}{i}\), and the complex has length \(n\): it stops at \(\Om^n_{X/k}\).

Example — affine space, in coordinates

On \(\Aa^n_k=\Spec k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), the module \(\Om^1\) is free on \(dx_1,\dots,dx_n\), and \(\Om^i\) is free on the \(\binom{n}{i}\) wedges \(dx_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{j_i}\). The differential is the familiar one, \(d(f)=\sum_i (\partial f/\partial x_i)\,dx_i\). A subtlety that will haunt us: in characteristic \(p\), \(d(x^p)=p\,x^{p-1}dx=0\). So \(x^p\) is a non-constant function with \(df=0\); \(p\)-th powers are "constants" for \(d\). This single fact is the seed of the Cartier isomorphism (Lesson 3).

Definition

The algebraic de Rham cohomology of \(X/k\) is the hypercohomology of the de Rham complex, \[ H^n_{\dR}(X/k) \;:=\; \mathbb{H}^n\!\left(X,\ \Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\right). \]

Why hypercohomology and not ordinary cohomology? Because we have a complex of sheaves, not a single sheaf, and we must remember both the cohomology of each \(\Om^i\) (a sheaf-theoretic, "vertical" datum) and the differential \(d\) linking them (a "horizontal" datum). Hypercohomology \(\mathbb{H}^n\) is the total cohomology that mixes the two. The standard tool for unmixing them is a spectral sequence, and there is one canonical choice.

The Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence

Filter the complex by its stupid (naive) filtration: let \(\sigma^{\geqslant i}\Om^{\bullet}\) be the subcomplex that agrees with \(\Om^{\bullet}\) in degrees \(\geqslant i\) and is zero below, \[ \sigma^{\geqslant i}\Om^{\bullet} : \; 0 \to \cdots \to 0 \to \Om^i \xto{d} \Om^{i+1} \to \cdots \] This is a subcomplex (applying \(d\) only raises degree, so it cannot escape downward), and the associated graded piece is a single sheaf placed in a single degree: \(\mathrm{gr}^i = \sigma^{\geqslant i}/\sigma^{\geqslant i+1} = \Om^i_{X/k}[-i]\). A filtered complex has a spectral sequence of hypercohomology, with \(E_1^{i,j}=\mathbb{H}^{i+j}(X,\mathrm{gr}^i)\). Here \(\mathbb{H}^{i+j}(X,\Om^i[-i]) = H^j(X,\Om^i)\), so we obtain:

Theorem (Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence)

For \(X\) smooth over \(k\) there is a convergent spectral sequence \[ \tag{0.1} E_1^{i,j} = H^j(X,\ \Om^i_{X/k}) \;\Longrightarrow\; H^{i+j}_{\dR}(X/k), \] with differentials \(d_r : E_r^{i,j}\to E_r^{i+r,\,j-r+1}\). On the bottom row \(j=0\), the map \(d_1 : E_1^{i,0}=H^0(X,\Om^i) \to E_1^{i+1,0}=H^0(X,\Om^{i+1})\) is induced by the de Rham differential \(d\).

Reading the \(E_1\) page

Picture \(E_1\) as a grid: column \(i\) holds the cohomology of \(\Om^i\), with \(H^j\) at height \(j\). The first differentials \(d_1\) point one column to the right (same height), and are exactly the de Rham differential acting on cohomology. The abutment \(H^n_{\dR}\) is assembled from the anti-diagonal \(i+j=n\). The two extreme cases are easy to name: \(E_1^{i,0}=H^0(\Om^i)\) is the space of global \(i\)-forms, and the bottom-row complex \((H^0(\Om^{\bullet}),d)\) is the complex of global forms with its own differential.

Degeneration and the universal Hodge inequality

Run the spectral sequence. Each page is the cohomology of the previous: \(E_{r+1}=H(E_r,d_r)\). Taking cohomology can only shrink dimensions, so \(\dim E_{r+1}^{i,j}\leqslant \dim E_r^{i,j}\), and in the limit \(\dim E_\infty^{i,j}\leqslant \dim E_1^{i,j}=h^{ij}\), where \(h^{ij}:=\dim_k H^j(X,\Om^i)\) are the Hodge numbers. Convergence says the abutment carries a finite filtration whose graded pieces are exactly the \(E_\infty^{i,j}\) with \(i+j=n\), so \(\dim H^n_{\dR} = \sum_{i+j=n}\dim E_\infty^{i,j}\). Combining:

\[ \dim_k H^n_{\dR}(X/k) \;=\; \sum_{i+j=n}\dim E_\infty^{i,j} \;\leqslant\; \sum_{i+j=n} h^{ij}. \tag{1.2} \]

The right-hand inequality (1.2) is unconditional: it holds for every smooth \(X/k\), in any characteristic, with no hypotheses whatsoever. The Hodge numbers always overcount de Rham cohomology. We say the spectral sequence degenerates at \(E_1\) if every differential \(d_r\) vanishes for \(r\geqslant 1\); equivalently \(E_1=E_\infty\); equivalently the inequalities in (1.2) are equalities for all \(n\):

Numerical criterion

The Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence degenerates at \(E_1\) if and only if \[ \sum_{i+j=n} h^{ij} \;=\; \dim_k H^n_{\dR}(X/k)\qquad\text{for every } n. \] This dimension equality is exactly the statement Deligne–Illusie prove (under hypotheses).

Theorem (classical; Hodge theory)

If \(X\) is smooth and proper over \(\C\) (or any field of characteristic \(0\)), the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence degenerates at \(E_1\).

Over \(\C\) this comes from Hodge theory: \(H^n_{\dR}(X^{\mathrm{an}})\cong\bigoplus_{i+j=n} H^j(X,\Om^i)\) by harmonic representatives. For \(X\) proper but not projective one reduces to the projective case by Chow's lemma and resolution of singularities. This is the analytic input that Deligne–Illusie replace by a purely algebraic argument running through characteristic \(p\) (Lesson 10).

In characteristic \(p\), degeneration can fail

The inequality (1.2) is universal, but equality is not. The cleanest way it breaks: suppose \(X\) carries a global \(1\)-form \(\omega\in H^0(X,\Om^1)\) that is not closed, \(d\omega\neq 0\). Then on the bottom row the differential \(d_1 : H^0(\Om^1)\to H^0(\Om^2)\) sends \([\omega]\mapsto[d\omega]\neq 0\), so \(d_1\neq 0\) and the sequence does not degenerate. Such non-closed global forms genuinely exist on smooth proper surfaces in characteristic \(p\); the first examples are Mumford's and Igusa's. We catalogue these failures — and exactly which hypothesis rules them out — in Lesson 14.

Worked examples

Example — the projective line \(\Pp^1\)

The complex is just \(\cO \xto{d} \Om^1\), with \(\Om^1_{\Pp^1}\cong\cO(-2)\). The four relevant cohomology groups are \[ H^0(\cO)=k,\quad H^1(\cO)=0,\qquad H^0(\Om^1)=0,\quad H^1(\Om^1)=k. \] (The last is Serre duality, \(H^1(\Om^1)\cong H^0(\cO)^\vee\).) So on the \(E_1\) page only \(E_1^{0,0}=k\) and \(E_1^{1,1}=k\) are nonzero; everything else vanishes. The only differential that could be nonzero, \(d_1:E_1^{0,0}\to E_1^{1,0}=H^0(\Om^1)=0\), lands in zero. Hence \(E_1=E_\infty\) and the sequence degenerates. Reading off the abutment: \[ H^0_{\dR}=k,\qquad H^1_{\dR}=0,\qquad H^2_{\dR}=k. \] Note \(h^{ij}=\delta_{ij}\) here — the Hodge diamond is a single diagonal.

Example — projective space \(\Pp^n\)

The standard computation of \(H^j(\Pp^n,\Om^i)\) (Bott's formula) gives \(h^{ij}=1\) if \(i=j\leqslant n\), and \(h^{ij}=0\) otherwise: the Hodge diamond is, again, the diagonal. Since the only nonzero \(E_1^{i,j}\) sit on the diagonal \(j=i\), every \(d_1\) (which shifts to column \(i+1\), same row) lands in a zero group; likewise all higher \(d_r\). The sequence degenerates, and the de Rham Betti numbers are \[ \dim H^{2m}_{\dR}(\Pp^n)=1\ (0\leqslant m\leqslant n),\qquad \dim H^{\mathrm{odd}}_{\dR}=0, \] with total dimension \(\sum_m \dim H^m_{\dR}=n+1\). (This is Exercise 1, and matches the topological Betti numbers of \(\Pp^n(\C)\).)

Example — an elliptic curve, and curves of genus \(g\)

Let \(E/k\) be a smooth proper curve of genus \(1\). The complex is \(\cO\xto{d}\Om^1\) with \(\Om^1_E\cong\cO_E\), so all four Hodge numbers are \(1\): \[ h^{00}=\dim H^0(\cO)=1,\quad h^{10}=\dim H^0(\Om^1)=1,\quad h^{01}=\dim H^1(\cO)=1,\quad h^{11}=\dim H^1(\Om^1)=1. \] The bottom-row differential \(d_1:H^0(\cO)\to H^0(\Om^1)\) is \(f\mapsto df\), and it kills the constants \(H^0(\cO)=k\) — every regular function on a proper curve is constant, with \(df=0\). So \(d_1=0\), the sequence degenerates, and \[ \dim H^1_{\dR}(E/k)=h^{10}+h^{01}=1+1=2,\qquad H^0_{\dR}=k,\quad H^2_{\dR}=k. \] The de Rham Betti numbers are \(1,2,1\). More generally, for a smooth proper curve of genus \(g\) one has \(h^{00}=h^{11}=1\) and \(h^{10}=h^{01}=g\), giving the key number \[ \dim H^1_{\dR}(X/k)=h^{10}+h^{01}=2g, \] which over \(\C\) is the topological first Betti number \(b_1=2g\).

Two cautions

Inequality vs. equality

Do not conflate the two halves of (1.2). The inequality \(\sum_{i+j=n}h^{ij}\geqslant \dim H^n_{\dR}\) is a formal consequence of having a spectral sequence and holds always. Equality — degeneration — is a real theorem: free over \(\C\), conditional in characteristic \(p\). The content of Deligne–Illusie is precisely a hypothesis (a lift mod \(p^2\), in dimension \(<p\)) that forces the equality.

Degeneration is not Hodge symmetry

Degeneration says the numbers \(h^{ij}\) add up correctly along anti-diagonals. It says nothing about the symmetry \(h^{ij}=h^{ji}\). Over \(\C\) that symmetry holds for a separate reason (complex conjugation on harmonic forms, \(H^j(\Om^i)\cong\overline{H^i(\Om^j)}\)). In characteristic \(p\) it can fail even when degeneration holds: there are smooth proper varieties with \(h^{ij}\neq h^{ji}\). Keep the two statements firmly apart (Lesson 14).

Exercises
  1. Assuming degeneration, compute all \(\dim H^m_{\dR}(\Pp^n/k)\) from the Hodge numbers \(h^{ij}=\delta_{ij}\) (for \(i,j\leqslant n\)). Confirm the total is \(n+1\) and compare with the topological Betti numbers of \(\Pp^n(\C)\).
  2. Prove the universal inequality \(\sum_{i+j=n} h^{ij}\geqslant \dim H^n_{\dR}(X/k)\) using only that \(\dim E_\infty^{i,j}\leqslant \dim E_1^{i,j}\) and that \(H^n_{\dR}\) is filtered with the \(E_\infty^{i,j}\) (\(i+j=n\)) as graded pieces. Identify precisely when equality holds for a fixed \(n\).
  3. Curves always degenerate, unconditionally. Let \(X\) be a smooth proper curve. Show that on the \(E_1\) page the only differential that is not automatically zero is the bottom-row \(d_1:E_1^{0,0}=H^0(\cO)\to E_1^{1,0}=H^0(\Om^1)\), and that the would-be next one \(d_1:H^0(\Om^1)\to H^0(\Om^2)=0\) vanishes because \(\Om^2=0\) on a curve. Then show \(d_1:H^0(\cO)\to H^0(\Om^1)\) is \(f\mapsto df=0\) (regular functions on a proper curve are constant). Conclude that the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence of any smooth proper curve degenerates at \(E_1\), with no lifting or characteristic hypothesis. (Foreshadowing: curves always lift to \(W_2(k)\), so the general theorem will reprove this — but here you get it by hand.)
  4. Over \(\C\), use the analytic comparison to identify \(H^n_{\dR}(X/\C)\) with singular cohomology \(H^n(X^{\mathrm{an}},\C)\), and the Hodge numbers \(h^{ij}\) with the classical Hodge numbers \(\dim H^{j}(X,\Om^i)=\dim H^{i,j}\). Re-derive the curve count \(b_1=2g\) and the elliptic-curve Betti numbers \(1,2,1\) from this point of view.
