5. Derived categories and decomposability

The Cartier isomorphism (Lesson 3) hands us the cohomology sheaves of \(F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\); it does not hand us the complex. Two complexes can have identical cohomology and still be utterly different objects of the derived category. The precise sense in which \(F_*\Om^{\bullet}\) is "no more complicated than its cohomology" is captured by a single word — decomposable — and the whole of Theorem 2.1 is the construction of a decomposition. This lesson is the homological dictionary for that word: the derived category at a working level, truncations and shifts, the definition of decomposability, and the two-term obstruction theory we will meet again, verbatim, in the gerbe theorem (Lesson 12). We end by showing — formally, in a few lines — why a decomposition forces Hodge–de Rham degeneration.

The derived category, at working level

Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be an abelian category (for us, \(\cO_X\)-modules on a scheme \(X\)). Start with complexes and chain maps; then formally invert the quasi-isomorphisms — the maps that are isomorphisms on every cohomology object. The result is the derived category \(D(\mathcal{A})\): same objects (complexes), but a morphism may now be a "zig-zag" of honest maps and inverses of quasi-isos. For a scheme we write \(D(X) := D(\cO_X\text{-Mod})\). The cohomology objects upgrade to functors \(\cH^i \colon D(\mathcal{A}) \to \mathcal{A}\). The one mental adjustment to make: an isomorphism in \(D(\mathcal{A})\) is an abstract identification, weaker than an isomorphism of complexes — it need not be induced by any single chain map.

We use \(D(\mathcal{A})\) as a black box with four properties. (i) \(\cH^i\) is a functor. (ii) A map is an isomorphism in \(D\) iff it is an isomorphism on every \(\cH^i\). (iii) \(\Hom_{D}(A, B[i]) = \Ext^i(A,B)\) for objects \(A,B\) of \(\mathcal{A}\) (so \(D\) computes \(\Ext\)). (iv) Every distinguished triangle \(A\to B\to C\xto{+1}A[1]\) yields long exact sequences both in \(\cH^i\) and, after applying \(\Hom_D(T,-)\) or \(\Hom_D(-,T)\), in \(\Hom\). That is all the machinery we need.

Truncations and shifts

Definition (truncation, §1.4)

For a complex \(L\) and an integer \(n\), the canonical truncation \(\tau_{\leqslant n}L\) is the subcomplex \[ (\tau_{\leqslant n}L)^i = \begin{cases} L^i & i<n,\\ \ker(d^n\colon L^n\to L^{n+1}) & i=n,\\ 0 & i>n,\end{cases} \] with the inherited differential and the inclusion \(\tau_{\leqslant n}L\hookrightarrow L\). Then \(\cH^i(\tau_{\leqslant n}L)=\cH^iL\) for \(i\leqslant n\) and \(0\) for \(i>n\). Write \(\tau_{<n}:=\tau_{\leqslant n-1}\). Dually, the quotient \(\tau_{\geqslant n}L\) has \((\tau_{\geqslant n}L)^i=0\) for \(i<n\), \(\operatorname{coker}(d^{n-1})\) for \(i=n\), and \(L^i\) for \(i>n\); it computes \(\cH^iL\) for \(i\geqslant n\).

These fit into a short exact sequence of complexes \(0\to\tau_{\leqslant n-1}L\to L\to \tau_{\geqslant n}L\to0\), hence a distinguished triangle \(\tau_{\leqslant n-1}L\to L\to \tau_{\geqslant n}L\xto{+1}\). The shift \(L[n]\) is \(L[n]^i=L^{i+n}\) with differential \((-1)^n d_L\). For an object \(M\) (a complex in degree \(0\)), \(M[n]\) is \(M\) placed in degree \(-n\); so \(\Om^i[-i]\) is \(\Om^i\) sitting in degree \(i\) — exactly the shape of the summands in Theorem 2.1.

Decomposability

Definition (§3.1)

An object \(K\in D^b(\mathcal{A})\) is decomposable if it is isomorphic in \(D(\mathcal{A})\) to a complex with zero differential — equivalently, to \(\bigoplus_i \cH^iK[-i]\). A decomposition is such an isomorphism.

Criterion

\(K\) is decomposable iff there exist morphisms \(f_i\colon \cH^iK[-i]\to K\) in \(D\) with \(\cH^i(f_i)=\mathrm{id}_{\cH^iK}\). The data \(f=\sum_i f_i\) is the decomposition.

Why: \(\bigoplus_i\cH^iK[-i]\) trivially admits such \(f_i\) (the inclusions of summands), so the condition is necessary. Conversely, given the \(f_i\), assemble \(f=\sum f_i\colon \bigoplus_i\cH^iK[-i]\to K\). On \(\cH^j\) the source has cohomology \(\cH^jK\) and \(f\) induces \(\cH^j(f_j)=\mathrm{id}\) (summands of other degrees contribute nothing). So \(f\) is a quasi-iso, i.e. an isomorphism in \(D\). \(\square\)

Example — over a field it is automatic

Take \(\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Vect}_k\). Every short exact sequence of vector spaces splits, so \(\Ext^i(V,W)=0\) for \(i\geqslant1\), and one checks every bounded complex is quasi-isomorphic to its cohomology with zero differential. Every bounded complex of vector spaces is decomposable. Decomposability is therefore a non-condition for vector spaces — it becomes a real question only for complexes of sheaves/modules, where \(\Ext^1\neq0\).

Decomposable is weaker than it looks, and harder than it looks

"Decomposable in \(D\)" asserts that an abstract isomorphism exists; it does not say the literal differential of \(F_*\Om^{\bullet}\) is zero (it is not), nor that the complex is formal on the nose. Conversely, the Cartier isomorphism gives us the \(\cH^i\) for free but produces no decomposition — knowing the cohomology never decomposes the complex. Manufacturing the maps \(f_i\) is the entire game (Lesson 6).

The two-term case: the obstruction \(e(K)\)

This is the crux for §3. Suppose \(K^i=0\) for \(i\neq0,1\). Then \(\tau_{\leqslant0}K=\cH^0K\) and \(\tau_{\geqslant1}K=\cH^1K[-1]\), and the truncation triangle reads \[ \cH^0K \;\longrightarrow\; K \;\longrightarrow\; \cH^1K[-1] \;\xto{+1}\; \cH^0K[1]. \] A decomposition is determined by a single \(f_1\colon \cH^1K[-1]\to K\) with \(\cH^1(f_1)=\mathrm{id}\) (then \(f_0\colon \cH^0K=\tau_{\leqslant0}K\hookrightarrow K\) is forced). The connecting map of the triangle is a canonical class \[ e(K)\in \Hom_{D}\!\bigl(\cH^1K[-1],\,\cH^0K[1]\bigr)=\Ext^2(\cH^1K,\cH^0K), \] and we claim: \(K\) is decomposable \(\iff e(K)=0\); and the set of decompositions, when nonempty, is an affine space (a torsor) under \(\Ext^1(\cH^1K,\cH^0K)\).

Apply \(\Hom_D(\cH^1K[-1],-)\) to the triangle. Using \(\Hom_D(A[m],B[n])=\Ext^{n-m}(A,B)\), the relevant stretch of the long exact sequence is \[ 0\to \Hom_D(\cH^1K[-1],\cH^0K)\to \Hom_D(\cH^1K[-1],K)\xto{\beta}\Hom_D(\cH^1K,\cH^1K)\xto{\partial}\Ext^2(\cH^1K,\cH^0K), \] where the leading \(0\) is \(\Ext^{-1}=0\), the map \(\beta\) sends \(f_1\mapsto\cH^1(f_1)\), and \(\partial(\mathrm{id})=e(K)\). A decomposition is exactly an element of \(\beta^{-1}(\mathrm{id})\). This is nonempty iff \(\mathrm{id}\) maps to \(0\) under \(\partial\), i.e. iff \(e(K)=0\); and then it is a coset of \(\ker\beta=\operatorname{im}\Hom_D(\cH^1K[-1],\cH^0K)\cong\Ext^1(\cH^1K,\cH^0K)\). \(\square\)

\(\Ext^2\) for existence, \(\Ext^1\) for the choice — the deformation pattern

Note the two groups are different: the obstruction \(e(K)\) lives in \(\Ext^2(\cH^1K,\cH^0K)\), while once it vanishes the decompositions form a torsor under \(\Ext^1(\cH^1K,\cH^0K)\). This is exactly Grothendieck's pattern in deformation theory — obstruction in \(H^2\), choices a torsor under \(H^1\) — and that is no accident (Lesson 4). For longer complexes \(e(K)=0\) is no longer sufficient: one genuinely needs all the \(f_i\) (equivalently, a tower of obstructions).

Example — \(K=\tau_{\leqslant1}F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\), and a coincidence

The simplest non-vector-space case is a two-term complex of sheaves \([\,\cO_X\xto{d}\Om^1_X\,]\) in degrees \(0,1\): here decomposability is a genuine question with obstruction in \(\Ext^2(\cH^1,\cH^0)\). Now take the truncation \(K=\tau_{\leqslant1} F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\). Cartier gives \(\cH^0K=\cO_{X'}\) and \(\cH^1K=\Om^1_{X'/k}\), so the decompositions of \(K\) form a torsor under \[ \Ext^1(\Om^1_{X'/k},\cO_{X'})=H^1(X',\mathcal{T}_{X'/k}), \] the very group that classifies lifts of \(X'\) over \(\Wt(k)\) (Lesson 4), with existence-obstruction in \(H^2(X',\mathcal{T}_{X'/k})\) — again the deformation obstruction. The two torsors are literally governed by the same cohomology. This coincidence, made into an equivalence of gerbes, is the content of Lesson 12: lifting \(X'\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) splitting \(\tau_{\leqslant1}F_*\Om^{\bullet}\).

A decomposition is data, not structure

Because it is a torsor under a nonzero group, a decomposition is not canonical: there is no preferred one. Different lifts mod \(p^2\) yield different decompositions (Lesson 8, Lesson 12). Cartier alone never selects a point of the torsor.

Why this gives degeneration

Suppose we have a decomposition \(F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\iso\bigoplus_i\Om^i_{X'/k}[-i]\) in \(D(X')\). Take hypercohomology. Because \(F\) is finite, hence affine, \(F_*\) is exact with \(R^qF_*=0\) for \(q>0\); so \(F_*\) computes hypercohomology and \[ \mathbb{H}^n(X',F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k})=\mathbb{H}^n(X,\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k})=H^n_{\dR}(X/k). \] On the other hand a shifted sheaf contributes \(\mathbb{H}^n(X',\Om^i_{X'}[-i])=H^{n-i}(X',\Om^i_{X'})\), so \[ H^n_{\dR}(X/k)\cong\bigoplus_i H^{n-i}(X',\Om^i_{X'/k}). \] Finally the Frobenius twist preserves Hodge numbers (eq. (2.3)): \(\dim H^{n-i}(X',\Om^i_{X'})=\dim H^{n-i}(X,\Om^i_X)=h^{\,i,\,n-i}\). Comparing dimensions, \[ \dim H^n_{\dR}(X/k)=\sum_{i+j=n}h^{ij}, \] which is precisely degeneration of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence at \(E_1\). The strategy of the paper is now exposed: prove \(F_*\Om^{\bullet}\) decomposes. (Theorem 2.1 only decomposes \(\tau_{<p}F_*\Om^{\bullet}\); this suffices when \(\dim X<p\), since then \(\Om^i=0\) for \(i\geqslant p\) and the truncation is everything — the dimension hypothesis of Cor. 2.4. The full count is Lesson 9.)

Exercises
  1. Verify the cohomology formulas \(\cH^i(\tau_{\leqslant n}L)=\cH^iL\) for \(i\leqslant n\) and \(0\) otherwise, directly from the definition, and check the shift sign convention: that \(d_{L[n]}=(-1)^nd_L\) is forced if \([1]\) is to be an additive functor with \((L[m])[n]=L[m+n]\).
  2. Prove that every bounded complex of \(k\)-vector spaces is decomposable, and that \(\Ext^i(V,W)=0\) for all \(i\geqslant1\). (Choose splittings of \(\ker d^i\hookrightarrow L^i\) and of \(d^i\); assemble a quasi-iso to \(\bigoplus\cH^iL[-i]\).)
  3. For \(K\) two-term, derive from the \(\Hom\) long exact sequence of the truncation triangle that the decompositions of \(K\), when nonempty, form a torsor under \(\Ext^1(\cH^1K,\cH^0K)\), and that \(e(K)=0\) is necessary and sufficient. Identify explicitly the term that makes the leading map injective.
  4. Carry out the hypercohomology dimension count above in full, justifying each isomorphism (exactness of \(F_*\); \(\mathbb{H}^n\) of a shifted sheaf; the twist identity (2.3)), and conclude degeneration from \(\sum_{i+j=n}h^{ij}\geqslant\dim H^n_{\dR}\) with equality.
  5. For \(K=\tau_{\leqslant1}F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\), identify the obstruction group \(\Ext^2(\Om^1_{X'},\cO_{X'})=H^2(X',\mathcal{T}_{X'})\) and the torsor group \(\Ext^1(\Om^1_{X'},\cO_{X'})=H^1(X',\mathcal{T}_{X'})\). Compare with the obstruction and torsor for lifting \(X'\) over \(\Wt(k)\) from Lesson 4, and state, as a conjecture, the equivalence proved in Lesson 12.
