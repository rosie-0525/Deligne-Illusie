10. From characteristic p to characteristic 0

Over \(\C\), degeneration of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence is a theorem of Hodge theory: harmonic representatives, the Kähler identities, the \(\partial\bar\partial\)-lemma. This lesson proves the same statement over any field of characteristic \(0\) with no analysis whatsoever. The mechanism is reduction modulo \(p\): a variety in characteristic \(0\) is "spread out" over a finitely generated ring, then reduced modulo a large prime — and, the small miracle, the reduction automatically satisfies both hypotheses of Corollary 2.4. Degeneration holds at the closed point and is transported back. The only ingredient beyond 2.4 is that coherent cohomology is well-behaved in families.

The statement

Corollary (2.7)

Let \(K\) be a field of characteristic \(0\) and \(X\) a proper smooth \(K\)-scheme. Then the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence \(E_1^{ij}=H^j(X,\Om^i_{X/K})\Rightarrow H^{i+j}_{\dR}(X/K)\) degenerates at \(E_1\).

By the warm-up of Lesson 0, degeneration is equivalent to the single family of numerical identities \[ \sum_{i+j=n} h^{ij} \;=\; h^n \qquad\text{for all } n, \qquad h^{ij}:=\dim_K H^j(X,\Om^i_{X/K}),\;\; h^n:=\dim_K H^n_{\dR}(X/K). \] Both sides are finite (properness), and "\(\leqslant\)" always holds; degeneration is exactly the reverse inequality, i.e. the equality. So the whole theorem is the assertion that finitely many integers agree — and integers, unlike harmonic forms, can be computed at a closed point of a scheme.

The philosophy: a constructible identity

The proof is a template Deligne uses repeatedly. An identity between dimensions of coherent cohomology is constructible: it is defined over a finitely generated subring of the base, and its truth is locally constant on the spectrum of that ring. Concretely, three facts do all the work. (i) Everything in sight is defined over a finitely generated \(\Z\)-algebra: \(X\), its sheaves \(\Om^i\), and the finitely many cohomology classes that witness the dimensions \(h^{ij},h^n\) involve only finitely many elements of \(K\). (ii) For a proper morphism the sheaves \(R^jf_*\Om^i\) and \(R^nf_*\Om^\bullet\) are coherent, hence locally free of constant rank after shrinking the base, and their formation commutes with base change there. (iii) Therefore the two sides of \(\sum h^{ij}=h^n\) are the ranks of two locally free sheaves on \(\Spec A\); if they agree at one well-chosen closed point, they agree generically, hence at the original characteristic-\(0\) point. The one closed point we choose lives in characteristic \(p\), where Corollary 2.4 is available for nothing.

Proof of Corollary 2.7

We may assume \(X\) is connected, of dimension \(d\).

  1. Reduce to \(K\) finitely generated over \(\Q\). The numbers \(h^{ij},h^n\) are unchanged under field extension (coherent cohomology commutes with the flat base change \(K\hookrightarrow \overline K\) or \(K\hookrightarrow K'\)), and \(X\), being of finite type, is already defined over a subfield \(K_0\subseteq K\) finitely generated over \(\Q\). Replace \(K\) by \(K_0\).
  2. Spread out. There exist an integral finite-type \(\Z\)-algebra \(A\), a proper smooth morphism \(f:\mathcal{X}\to\Spec A\) of relative dimension \(d\), and a ring map \(A\to K\) with \(X=\mathcal{X}\otimes_A K\). (Clear denominators in the finitely many equations defining \(\mathcal{X}\) and the data establishing properness and smoothness.)
  3. Achieve constant ranks. The sheaves \(R^jf_*\Om^i_{\mathcal{X}/A}\) and \(R^nf_*\Om^\bullet_{\mathcal{X}/A}\) are coherent. After inverting a suitable nonzero \(s\in A\) (shrinking \(\Spec A\) to a dense open), they become locally free of constant ranks \(h^{ij}\) and \(h^n\), with formation compatible with arbitrary base change. (Generic freeness for the first; for the second, the de Rham complex has locally free terms, so its hypercohomology is computed by a perfect complex whose cohomology is locally free over the same open.)
  4. Choose a good closed point. Let \(T\subseteq\Spec A\) be the closure of a closed point of \(\Spec(A\otimes\Q)\); then \(T\) is quasi-finite and flat over \(\Spec\Z\). Pick a closed point \(s\in T\) at which \(T\) is étale over \(\Z\) and whose residue field \(k:=k(s)\) is finite of characteristic \(p\geqslant d\) (all but finitely many \(p\) qualify, and none of them divides \(s\)). Étaleness over \(\Z\) means the local ring \(\cO_{s}\) of \(T\) at \(s\) is unramified: its maximal ideal is \(\mathfrak{m}_s=(p)\), and \[ \cO_{s}/\mathfrak{m}_s^2 \;=\; \cO_{s}/(p^2)\;\cong\; \Wt(k). \] Hence \(\mathcal{X}\otimes_A\bigl(\cO_{s}/\mathfrak{m}_s^2\bigr)\) is a proper smooth scheme over \(\Wt(k)\) reducing modulo \(p\) to the special fibre \(\mathcal{X}_s:=\mathcal{X}\otimes_A k\): that is, a lift of \(\mathcal{X}_s\) over \(\Wt(k)\) (Lesson 4).
  5. Apply 2.4 and read back. \(\mathcal{X}_s\) is proper smooth over \(k\) of dimension \(d\leqslant p\) and admits a lift over \(\Wt(k)\); by Corollary 2.4 its Hodge–de Rham sequence degenerates, i.e. \(\sum_{i+j=n}\dim_k H^j(\mathcal{X}_s,\Om^i)=\dim_k H^n_{\dR}(\mathcal{X}_s/k)\) for all \(n\). By the constant-rank/base-change of step 3, the left summands equal \(h^{ij}\) and the right side equals \(h^n\). Therefore \(\sum_{i+j=n}h^{ij}=h^n\) for \(X\), which is exactly degeneration over \(K\). \(\;\blacksquare\)
The magic: both hypotheses, for free

Corollary 2.4 asks for two things — a lift mod \(p^2\) and dimension \(\leqslant p\) — and naively each looks hard to arrange. Reduction modulo a large prime delivers both at no cost. The lift exists because \(\mathcal{X}\) is defined over \(A\), which already contains the \(\Wt(k)\)-thickening \(\cO_{s}/\mathfrak{m}_s^2\): the family is its own lift. And the dimension bound \(d\leqslant p\) holds because we were free to choose \(p\geqslant d\), there being infinitely many primes to pick from. Nothing analytic, nothing transcendental: the geometry of the single object \(\mathcal{X}\to\Spec A\) supplies the lift, and the abundance of primes supplies the inequality.

Examples

Example — the clean illustration: a smooth hypersurface

Let \(X\subset\Pp^{n}_{\C}\) be a smooth hypersurface of degree \(e\), so \(d=\dim X=n-1\). Its defining polynomial has coefficients \(a_\bullet\) generating a finite extension of \(\Q\); spread out over \[ A=\Z[a_\bullet]\bigl[\tfrac1\Delta\bigr],\qquad \Delta=\text{the discriminant}, \] with \(\mathcal{X}\subset\Pp^{n}_A\) the same hypersurface. Smoothness over \(\Spec A\) is exactly \(\Delta\in A^\times\). Now reduce modulo any prime \(p\) with \(p>d\) and \(p\nmid\Delta\): the fibre \(\mathcal{X}_p\subset\Pp^n_{\Fp}\) is again a smooth hypersurface, of dimension \(d<p\). Its lift is utterly transparent — the very same equation read over \(\Wt(\Fp)=\Z/p^2\) defines a smooth hypersurface over \(\Z/p^2\). Apply 2.4. Hypersurfaces make the "lift mod \(p^2\)" hypothesis visibly free: the equation never knew it was in characteristic \(p\).

Example — elliptic curve, and curves of genus \(g\)

For \(E/\C\) an elliptic curve, \(d=1\), and for all but finitely many \(p\) the spreading-out has good reduction \(E_p/\Fp\), a smooth proper curve. Curves always lift to \(\Wt\) (the obstruction sits in \(H^2\) of a curve, which vanishes — Lesson 4), and any \(p\geqslant 2\geqslant d+1\) works, so 2.4 applies and recovers \(\dim H^1_{\dR}=h^{10}+h^{01}=1+1=2\). More generally a smooth proper curve of genus \(g\) over \(\C\) gives \(\dim H^1_{\dR}=2g\) by the identical route. (Here the route is overkill: curves degenerate unconditionally — Lesson 9 — but they are the reassuring smallest case.)

Subtleties

Why properness is essential

Properness is what makes \(R^jf_*\Om^i\) coherent (so the ranks \(h^{ij}\) are finite and base-change holds) — the engine of steps 3 and 5. Drop it and the cohomologies can be infinite-dimensional and jump wildly in families; the argument collapses. For open or singular \(X\) one passes to a smooth compactification with normal-crossings boundary and the logarithmic de Rham complex, where a parallel statement holds (Lesson 13).

Why a large prime, and why only finitely many are lost

The condition \(p\geqslant d\) is precisely what lands the special fibre in the range "dimension \(\leqslant p\)" of 2.4; this is the only place the size of \(p\) is used. We discard exactly the primes dividing the inverted element \(s\) (equivalently the discriminant), the primes of bad reduction, and the finitely many \(p<d\). All of these together are a finite set, and infinitely many good primes remain — one is all we need.

This gives degeneration, not Hodge symmetry

The algebraic proof yields \(\sum_{i+j=n}h^{ij}=h^n\) but says nothing about the symmetry \(h^{ij}=h^{ji}\). That symmetry is genuinely complex-analytic (complex conjugation on harmonic forms); in characteristic \(p\) it can fail outright (Lesson 14). So even in characteristic \(0\) this route recovers strictly less than full Hodge theory: degeneration is the part that survives reduction mod \(p\); symmetry is the part that does not.

A robust general technique

"Spread out over a finitely generated \(\Z\)-algebra, specialize to a closed point of large residue characteristic, transport the conclusion back" is a method, not a trick. It moves an algebraic identity between characteristic \(0\) and characteristic \(p\) in both directions, and Deligne deploys it elsewhere (e.g. Weil-II style arguments, and his proof of the Weil conjectures' applications). The content of this lesson is that the Hodge–de Rham degeneration is robust enough to make the trip.

Exercises
  1. Spell out the spreading-out for a single smooth plane curve \(C=\{F(x,y)=0\}\subset\Aa^2_K\) (or its projective closure): exhibit \(A\), the element \(s\) one inverts, and the family \(\mathcal{C}\to\Spec A\). What is \(s\) explicitly?
  2. Explain carefully why only finitely many primes are excluded. (List every place a prime can be "bad": dividing \(s\); below \(d\); where some \(R^jf_*\Om^i\) fails to be free. Why is each set finite?)
  3. Pinpoint where "\(p\geqslant d\)" enters the proof, and describe what would go wrong if one specialized to a small prime \(p<d\). (Which hypothesis of 2.4 fails, and why can the spectral sequence then genuinely have nonzero differentials?)
  4. Carry out the hypersurface example for a smooth quartic surface \(X\subset\Pp^3_{\C}\): here \(d=2\), so any \(p\geqslant 3\) (avoiding \(p\mid\Delta\)) works. Identify \(A\), exhibit the lift over \(\Z/p^2\), and conclude degeneration. (You may take the Fermat quartic \(x_0^4+x_1^4+x_2^4+x_3^4=0\), where the bad primes are visibly only \(p=2\).)
  5. Why does verifying degeneration for a single small prime \(p\) not let you conclude in characteristic \(0\)? (Hint: if \(p<d\), the special fibre lies outside the range of 2.4 and may fail to degenerate — its ranks could be strictly smaller — so equality at that fibre is neither available nor, if it held, transportable along the family. Constancy of ranks transports information from a good fibre to the generic point, not from a bad one.)
