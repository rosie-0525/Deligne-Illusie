14. Assumptions, subtleties, and counterexamples

The main theorem (Lesson 0) is hedged with hypotheses: a perfect field of characteristic \(p\); a lift mod \(p^2\); dimension \(\leqslant p\); the range \(i<p\); the lift is of \(X'\), not \(X\); properness; ampleness in the vanishing theorem. This capstone is the "why is every hypothesis there" reference. For each one we give either a counterexample if it is dropped, or an explanation of why it sits exactly where it does. One lesson recurs throughout, and it is the moral of the whole course: liftability mod \(p^2\) is sufficient but not necessary for degeneration and vanishing; the genuine obstruction is not cohomological but lives in the derived category (Lesson 12). The cohomological consequences are only its shadows, and a shadow can vanish while the body remains.

Failures: in characteristic \(p\), these theorems are genuinely false

It is worth saying plainly, before the apologetics, that none of the conclusions hold unconditionally over a field of characteristic \(p\). The hypotheses are not bookkeeping; without them the statements are wrong, with explicit witnesses.

Degeneration fails: Mumford's non-closed forms

Mumford constructed smooth projective surfaces over an algebraically closed field \(k\) of characteristic \(p\) carrying a global \(1\)-form that is not closed: some \(\omega\in H^0(X,\Om^1_{X/k})\) has \(d\omega\neq 0\). Further such examples are due to Igusa, Lang, and Raynaud–Szpiro. A non-closed \(\omega\) is exactly a nonzero element in the source of the bottom-row differential of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence, \[ d_1 : H^0(X,\Om^1_{X/k})\longrightarrow H^0(X,\Om^2_{X/k}), \] since on global sections this \(d_1\) is induced by the de Rham \(d\) (Lesson 1). So \(d_1\neq 0\): the spectral sequence does not degenerate at \(E_1\). By the contrapositive of Corollary 2.4 (Lesson 9) — degeneration in dimension \(\leqslant p\) for liftable \(X\) — these surfaces do not lift over \(W_2(k)\). Non-liftability is here detected purely by a differential on forms.

The \(p=2\) Enriques witness

For \(p=2\), the Enriques surfaces "of type \(\alpha_2\)" give a different failure in a different slot of the same spectral sequence: there \[ d_1 : H^1(X,\cO_X)\longrightarrow H^1(X,\Om^1_{X/k}) \] is nonzero (Ref. 2.6(i)). Both witnesses live at \(p\leqslant\dim X=2\), precisely the boundary where Corollary 2.4 still claims to apply if the surface lifts — so, again, they cannot lift mod \(p^2\).

Kodaira vanishing fails: Raynaud–Szpiro

Raynaud and Szpiro (Remark 2.10(ii)) built smooth projective surfaces \(X/k\) carrying an ample invertible sheaf \(\cL\) with \[ H^1(X,\cL^{-1})\neq 0. \] So Kodaira vanishing — true over \(\C\) by Hodge theory — is simply false in characteristic \(p\). These surfaces necessarily do not lift mod \(p^2\): otherwise Raynaud's Corollary 2.8 (Lesson 11) would force the vanishing. The contrast with 2.8 is exactly the liftability hypothesis; see the table below.

Hodge symmetry can fail even when the SS degenerates

Degeneration is strictly weaker than complex Hodge theory. Even for an \(X/k\) whose Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence does degenerate at \(E_1\), the symmetry \(h^{ij}=h^{ji}\) of Hodge numbers need not hold (Remark 2.6(ii); cf. Serre's examples). The algebraic method delivers the additive relation \(\sum_{i+j=n}h^{ij}=\dim H^n_{\dR}\) and nothing more — it never invokes a complex conjugation, so it cannot and does not produce symmetry. Anyone expecting "Hodge theory in characteristic \(p\)" should read this as the precise boundary of what is on offer.

Why each bound sits exactly where it does

The range \(i<p\) is real, not cosmetic

The passage from \(\varphi^1\) to the higher \(\varphi^i\) (Lesson 7) uses the antisymmetrization that divides by \(i!\); the map \(\varphi^i=\tfrac{1}{i!}\bigwedge^i\varphi^1\) exists precisely because \(i!\) is invertible mod \(p\), i.e. precisely when \(i<p\). At \(i=p\) the factorial \(p!\equiv 0\) and the construction breaks: there is no candidate splitting in degree \(p\). This is why Theorem 2.1 splits only the truncation \(\tau_{<p}F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\), and never claims more. The truncation is structural, not a defect of the proof.

The bound \(\dim\leqslant p\), and why \(\dim>p\) is the frontier

Three regimes. For \(\dim X<p\) the de Rham complex sits inside the splittable range and degeneration is immediate from Theorem 2.1. For \(\dim X=p\) one needs an extra input — Serre (equivalently Grothendieck) duality together with the vanishing of the top piece \(H^{p+1}\) — to push the dimension count through (Lesson 9). For \(\dim X>p\) the method stops: Deligne and Illusie have neither a proof nor a counterexample that \(F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\) decomposes — or even that the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence degenerates — for a proper liftable \(X\) of dimension \(\geqslant p+1\) (Remark 2.6(iii)). This is an honest open problem and the precise frontier of the technique: a lift mod \(p^2\) controls the complex only up to degree \(p\), and beyond that the torsion sheaves \(\mathscr{H}^i\) for \(i\geqslant p\) are simply not reached by the construction.

Why exactly mod \(p^2\) — \(W_2\), and no less

The key computation (Lesson 6) divides by \(p\) once: from a lift \(\tF\) of Frobenius one forms \(\tfrac1p\tF^{*}\), legal precisely because \(\tF^{*}\) lands in \(p\cdot(\text{flat module})\). A lift mod \(p\) alone is no help — \(F^{*}=0\) on differentials, carrying no information. A lift mod \(p^2\) is just enough to make the single division meaningful. Higher \(W_n(k)\) is not needed for the main results; it yields refinements (Witt-divided variants, Remark 2.2) but the theorems already hold over \(W_2\). So "mod \(p^2\)" is the minimal exact hypothesis, neither weakenable to mod \(p\) nor requiring all of \(W(k)\).

The roles of "proper" and "ample"

Two analytic-looking words earn their place cheaply. Properness is used only to make coherent cohomology finite-dimensional, so that the additive count "Hodge numbers sum to Betti numbers" is a statement about finite integers at all. Ampleness enters the vanishing theorem (Lesson 11) in exactly one place: to seed Serre vanishing \(H^j(X,\cF\otimes\cL^{n})=0\) for \(n\gg0\), against which the Frobenius descent runs. The descent itself is formal; ampleness is the seed, not the engine.

Sufficient, not necessary: the obstruction lives in \(D(X')\)

Raynaud's surface: degeneration and vanishing without a lift

This is the sharpest cautionary tale (Remark 2.10(iii)). For \(p\geqslant7\), by the Godeaux–Serre method, there is a smooth projective surface \(X/\Fp\) that does not lift over \(\Z/p^2\), yet for which

  • (a) the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence degenerates at \(E_1\); and
  • (b) every ample invertible sheaf satisfies Kodaira–Akizuki–Nakano vanishing.

So the "pathology" of non-liftability is completely invisible at the level of the spectral sequence and of vanishing theorems: both cohomological consequences hold, although the sufficient condition that the paper uses to prove them fails. What, then, actually goes wrong? By the gerbe theorem (Corollary 3.6, Lesson 12), \[ X' \text{ lifts over } W_2(k) \iff \tau_{\leqslant 1}F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\ \text{is decomposable in } D(X'). \] For Raynaud's surface the right-hand side fails: \(\tau_{\leqslant 1}F_*\Om^{\bullet}_{X/k}\) is not decomposable, even though its cohomology adds up correctly. The failure is real and detectable — but only in the derived category, not in any of its numerical shadows.

The lift is of \(X'\), not \(X\)

Throughout, the hypothesis is liftability of the Frobenius twist \(X'\) (Lesson 2), not of \(X\). Over a perfect field \(k\), Frobenius is an automorphism of \(k\) and \(X'\cong X^{(p)}\) is abstractly isomorphic to a base change of \(X\), so the distinction is invisible and one harmlessly says "\(X\) lifts." In a family over a base \(S\) of characteristic \(p\) this collapses: \(X\) and \(X'\) are genuinely different \(S\)-schemes, and to convert a lift of \(X/S\) into a lift of \(X'/S\) one needs a lift \(\tF_S\) of the absolute Frobenius of \(S\) (Remark 4.1.6, Lesson 13). Forgetting the prime is safe only over a perfect base.

The decomposition is not canonical

A lift mod \(p^2\) determines a splitting \(\varphi_{\tX}\), but the splitting depends on the lift. Two lifts generally give two different decompositions, and the set of decompositions of \(\tau_{\leqslant 1}\) is a torsor under \(H^1(X',\Theta_{X'/k})\) (Lesson 8, Lesson 12) — the same group that controls deformations. There is no preferred splitting in the absence of a preferred lift; the isomorphism of Theorem 2.1 is canonical given \(\tX\), and only given \(\tX\).

Where this sits in the history

Degeneration in characteristic \(0\) was classically extracted from Hodge theory. The first proof avoiding harmonic analysis was Faltings', through \(p\)-adic Hodge theory (Hodge–Tate decompositions); Kato, and then Fontaine–Messing, proved degeneration in characteristic \(p\) under liftability by crystalline methods, recovering the characteristic-\(0\) statement. Deligne and Illusie's contribution is the elementary and more precise object — the explicit splitting \(\varphi_{\tX}\) — from which degeneration, its descent to characteristic \(0\) (Lesson 10), and Kodaira–Akizuki–Nakano vanishing all follow by formal arguments and dimension counts. The seed, as they note, is an observation of Mazur, made explicit in work of Ogus and Kato.

Exercises
  1. Show that a non-closed global \(1\)-form \(\omega\in H^0(X,\Om^1)\) forces the bottom-row \(d_1\) of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence to be nonzero, hence non-degeneration, hence (which theorem's contrapositive?) non-liftability of \(X\) over \(W_2(k)\) in dimension \(\leqslant p\). Name the theorem and state the contrapositive precisely.
  2. Explain in what sense Raynaud's surface (Remark 2.10(iii)) is consistent with Corollary 3.6: the spectral sequence degenerates and KAN holds, yet what the gerbe theorem asserts to fail is the decomposability of \(\tau_{\leqslant 1}F_*\Om^{\bullet}\) — a property invisible to the spectral sequence. Why does degeneration not imply decomposability?
  3. Reconcile Remark 2.10(ii) (Kodaira vanishing fails: an ample \(\cL\) with \(H^1(X,\cL^{-1})\neq0\)) with Corollary 2.8 (KAN holds). Which single hypothesis distinguishes the two situations, and what does its presence guarantee?
  4. Locate, precisely, where the dimension bound enters the proof of degeneration, distinguishing \(\dim<p\) from \(\dim=p\). Why is \(\dim>p\) open — what does a lift mod \(p^2\) fail to control?
  5. Give the minimal-hypothesis argument that the lift must be mod \(p^2\) and no less: explain why mod \(p\) carries no information, why one division by \(p\) suffices, and why \(W_n\) for \(n\geqslant3\) is unnecessary for the main theorems.
  6. Explain why the method does not yield Hodge symmetry \(h^{ij}=h^{ji}\): which ingredient of the complex-analytic proof has no characteristic-\(p\) counterpart here, and what does the algebraic argument deliver in its place?
